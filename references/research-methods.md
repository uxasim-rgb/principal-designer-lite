# Research methods and instruments

56 entries. Use this selector with references/research-strategy.md. The table is a scope and selection guide. Advanced methods and instruments require their original protocol, assumptions, relevant expertise, and appropriate validation before use. No sample-size recipe applies to every method or decision.

| Method/instrument | Question | Execution focus | Limit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Desk/evidence review | What is already known? | Inventory sources, provenance, recency, applicability, contradictions, and gaps. | Existing material may not answer this decision. |
| Competitive/substitute review | How else is the need served? | Compare observable tasks, alternatives, workarounds, and evidence. | Competitor behavior does not prove usability. |
| Stakeholder interviews | What constraints and beliefs shape the service? | Interview decision and operational owners; separate beliefs from user evidence. | Stakeholders are not automatic user proxies. |
| Domain expert interviews | What tacit rules and critical exceptions matter? | Elicit terminology, cases, decision rules, and authoritative references. | Validate important rules with the accountable source. |
| User interviews | How do people describe real experience? | Ask neutral questions about specific past events; probe context and consequences. | Stated behavior may differ from actual behavior. |
| Contextual inquiry | How is work actually performed? | Observe real tasks in context and probe actions without excessive disruption. | Observation can affect behavior and access. |
| Field observation/ethnography | What social and environmental context matters? | Define observation focus, field access, reflexivity, ethics, and synthesis. | Requires contextual competence and sufficient fieldwork. |
| Diary study | How do experiences evolve outside sessions? | Plan prompts, cadence, accessible capture, participant burden, and follow-up. | Attrition and self-report bias need attention. |
| Longitudinal study | What changes over repeated use? | Track relevant users and tasks across meaningful time points. | Cohort change and attrition can distort interpretation. |
| Survey | How are perceptions or reported behaviors distributed? | Define population, sampling, neutral questions, response options, pilot, and analysis. | Convenience responses do not establish population prevalence. |
| Intercept research | What is happening at a specific moment? | Recruit or ask in context with minimal interruption and appropriate consent. | The intercepted audience can be systematically different. |
| Product analytics | What instrumented behavior occurs? | Verify event definitions, identity, cohorts, context, and missing data. | Logs do not directly reveal intent or causation. |
| Search-log analysis | What are people trying to find? | Inspect queries, reformulation, relevance, zero results, and outcomes. | Queries can contain sensitive data and ambiguous intent. |
| Support/contact analysis | What problems reach support? | Sample and code contacts, consequences, attempted recovery, and affected tasks. | People who contact support are a selected subset. |
| Review/community analysis | What feedback is publicly expressed? | Inspect source, context, authenticity, recurring needs, and contradictions. | Public feedback is not representative by default. |
| Open card sorting | How might people group and label information? | Use representative items, neutral tasks, participant rationale, and cluster interpretation. | Results are inputs to IA, not a final sitemap. |
| Closed card sorting | Do proposed categories make sense? | Ask participants to place items into defined categories and explain difficulty. | It does not directly test actual navigation. |
| Hybrid card sorting | Where do proposed categories break down? | Allow predefined and participant-created categories. | Interpret mixed structures carefully. |
| Tree testing | Can users find information in a hierarchy? | Define task destinations, success/directness, paths, and representative labels. | Visual design and search may be absent from the test. |
| First-click testing | Does the initial action lead toward a task? | Use representative tasks and starting states; analyze destination and rationale. | One click does not validate the whole task. |
| Moderated usability testing | Can people complete realistic tasks and recover? | Prepare neutral tasks, observe behavior, probe without leading, capture outcomes. | Moderator help and prototype limits affect results. |
| Unmoderated usability testing | How do participants attempt tasks independently? | Pilot instructions, prototype paths, capture, recruitment, and data-quality checks. | Limited opportunity for clarification or follow-up. |
| Comparative usability benchmark | How do task outcomes compare across designs? | Use comparable participants, tasks, environments, measures, and order handling. | Learning and order effects can invalidate comparison. |
| Heuristic evaluation | Where might expert review identify problems? | Inspect tasks/states using relevant heuristics, evidence, and severity rationale. | Expert judgment is not observed user evidence. |
| Cognitive walkthrough | Can a defined user discover the next step? | Inspect goals, available cues, action selection, and feedback at each step. | Depends on accurate assumptions about user knowledge. |
| Pluralistic walkthrough | What issues emerge across perspectives? | Walk tasks with relevant users, designers, engineers, and operators. | Group influence requires deliberate facilitation. |
| Participatory/co-design | What can people create or reframe together? | Plan inclusive activities, materials, facilitation, and evidence capture. | Generated ideas remain unvalidated concepts. |
| Concept testing | Does a proposed approach address a meaningful need? | Use clear, comparable concepts and probe understanding, value, and concerns. | Interest does not establish future behavior or viability. |
| Desirability/visual reaction study | How is visual character perceived? | Use controlled stimuli, clear questions, representative audiences, and context. | Preference does not establish task success. |
| Content comprehension testing | Do people understand important meaning? | Ask participants to explain instructions, choices, status, and consequences. | Do not reward guessing or paraphrase alone. |
| Cloze testing | How well is a specific text understood? | Verify the protocol, language suitability, selection of omissions, and scoring. | Not a universal reading-level or usability test. |
| Eye tracking | Where and when is gaze recorded? | Plan calibration, tasks, apparatus, measures, data quality, and interpretation. | Gaze location does not directly reveal comprehension. |
| Click/scroll aggregate analysis | What recorded interaction patterns occur? | Inspect instrumentation, exposure, device context, cohorts, and normalization. | A heatmap does not explain motivation. |
| Session replay review | What recorded sequences may reveal friction? | Use appropriate permission, masking, sampling, coding, and event context. | Privacy and recording gaps limit conclusions. |
| Controlled A/B experiment | What is the effect of a controlled change? | Specify hypothesis, assignment, exposure, measures, power, guardrails, and analysis. | Causal validity depends on design and execution. |
| Multivariate experiment | How do multiple factors and interactions behave? | Specify factors, assignment, interaction questions, sample needs, and analysis. | Complexity and traffic requirements can be substantial. |
| Quasi-experimental evaluation | What can be learned without random assignment? | Select an appropriate comparison strategy and state identifying assumptions. | Causal claims remain assumption-dependent. |
| Wizard-of-Oz prototype | How might a not-yet-automated interaction work? | Plan operator behavior, consistency, ethics, debriefing, and feasibility limits. | Simulated capability does not establish technical feasibility. |
| Concierge/service pilot | Does a manually delivered service solve the need? | Define service steps, human effort, outcomes, cost, and boundaries. | Manual success may not transfer to automation or scale. |
| Technical feasibility spike | Can a critical behavior be implemented? | Build the smallest representative implementation with real constraints. | A technical demo does not prove user value. |
| Formative human-factors evaluation | Which use difficulties require design change? | Study representative critical tasks, users, and environments with qualified review. | Domain methods and risk analysis govern suitability. |
| Summative/validation human-factors study | Does the final intended use meet its evaluation goals? | Use the applicable specialist protocol, representative conditions, and documented evidence. | Do not infer compliance from a generic usability study. |
| Accessibility evaluation | What barriers exist against the applicable target? | Combine design/manual/automated/assistive evaluation and scoped evidence. | A single tool does not establish conformance. |
| Research with disabled users | How do actual access needs affect the task? | Recruit relevant people, accommodate participation, and study actual setups. | One participant cannot represent every disability. |
| Service prototyping/roleplay | How do channels and people work together? | Simulate frontstage/backstage actions, handoffs, exceptions, and recovery. | Simulation may omit operational pressure. |
| Field pilot/beta | What happens under limited real operation? | Define coverage, exposure, monitoring, feedback, support, and graduation criteria. | Early adopters may differ from later audiences. |
| MaxDiff research | What relative priorities are expressed? | Verify the choice design, sampling, analysis, and interpretation with expertise. | Relative preference does not directly predict behavior. |
| Conjoint research | How are attribute tradeoffs expressed? | Verify attribute/level design, realistic tasks, sampling, and statistical model. | Results depend on the stated-choice design. |
| Pricing/willingness research | How are price and value understood? | Choose an appropriate method and verify its assumptions and context. | Stated willingness can differ from actual purchase. |
| Jobs-to-be-done interview | What progress and switching forces matter? | Explore actual situations, alternatives, triggers, constraints, and outcomes. | Avoid imposing a framework on every response. |
| System Usability Scale | How is perceived usability measured with this instrument? | Obtain the original instrument, scoring, administration, and interpretation guidance. | Verify scoring; its score is not a percent of tasks completed. |
| Single Ease Question | How difficult did this task feel? | Verify original wording, timing, scale, and interpretation. | A single rating does not diagnose the cause. |
| UMUX/UMUX-LITE | How is perceived usability measured briefly? | Verify the exact version, wording, scoring, and intended use. | Do not mix versions or unsupported conversions. |
| NASA-TLX | What perceived workload is reported? | Use the instrument owner's instructions and chosen administration variant. | Workload includes multiple dimensions; avoid universal difficulty cutoffs. |
| Triangulation/evidence synthesis | Do different sources support a conclusion? | Compare questions, populations, methods, contexts, contradictions, and evidence strength. | Agreement among biased sources can still mislead. |
| Systematic/scoping evidence review | What does a defined literature set support? | Specify question, search strategy, criteria, screening, extraction, quality appraisal, and limits. | Do not call a casual search a systematic review. |

### Selection protocol

1. State the decision and uncertainty.
2. Identify whether the question concerns behavior, perception, distribution, change, cause, technical feasibility, or context.
3. Check existing evidence, product stage, access, time, risk, and required precision.
4. Choose an appropriate method or complementary combination; explain the choice.
5. Define population, sampling, accommodations, data handling, analysis and limitations.
6. Pilot the protocol and study materials.
7. Conduct only the actual authorized research possible in this environment.
8. Report observed evidence and uncertainty; otherwise deliver a plan and clearly labeled hypotheses.

### Core playbook: Interview

Use actual experiences and specific examples. Ask neutral questions, probe context and consequences, seek contrary cases, avoid selling the solution, record consent and evidence, and distinguish participant statements from observed behavior. Synthesize patterns without inventing prevalence.

### Core playbook: Usability test

Define representative tasks, starting states and completion criteria. Pilot the prototype and capture. Observe behavior, avoid helping too early, record errors and recovery, probe neutrally, and report prototype and sample limits. Separate behavior, self-reported ease and visual preference.

### Core playbook: Survey

Define the population and intended inference, choose a defensible sampling strategy, write neutral items with suitable response options, test comprehension and accessibility, plan analysis before collection, and report nonresponse and coverage limitations. Use numerical claims only within what the sampling permits.

### Core playbook: IA evaluation

Use card sorting for organization and category understanding, tree tests for hierarchy-based finding, and realistic navigation tasks for the integrated experience. Choose representative content and tasks. Account for search, visual cues and context omitted by a simplified test.

### Core playbook: Quantitative benchmark

Define task success, errors, time, effort and attitudinal measures. Preserve comparability of participants, tasks, devices, content and conditions. Plan uncertainty and sample requirements with appropriate statistical support. Do not treat every observed difference as meaningful or causal.

### Core playbook: Instrument use

Retrieve the exact instrument, version, wording, timing, scoring, administration variant, usage conditions and interpretation. Do not alter standardized items casually or invent benchmarks.
