# Portfolio case studies and presenting work

A case study shows how you think and what changed because of your work. Hiring managers skim first, then read the parts that interest them, so structure for both.

## Structure

1. **Title and one-line summary.** What the product is and the outcome. "Redesigning invoice approval for a 40-person finance team, cutting approval time from days to hours" beats "Project Atlas".
2. **Context strip.** Role, team, timeline, platform, your specific responsibilities. Be precise about what you did versus the team.
3. **The problem.** Who was struggling with what, and how you knew. Evidence, not adjectives.
4. **Constraints.** Technical, business, time, regulatory. Constraints make decisions interesting.
5. **Process, selectively.** Show the 2 or 3 moments where a decision was made: what options existed, what evidence you used, why you chose. Skip generic process diagrams (empathize, define, ideate...) that every portfolio has.
6. **The solution.** Large, real visuals. Annotate key decisions on the screens.
7. **Outcome.** Measured results if you have them, with the basis. If you do not, say what was shipped, what feedback came back and what you would measure. Do not invent percentages.
8. **Reflection.** What you would do differently, what you learned. One short paragraph.

## Writing

- First person, past tense, plain words. "I interviewed 8 approvers" not "Leveraging user-centric methodologies...".
- Every section adds new information; do not repeat the summary in the problem in the outcome.
- Short paragraphs, descriptive subheadings so a skimmer gets the story from headings and images alone.
- Avoid em dash heavy prose and AI phrasing (see `anti-slop.md`).

## Visuals

- Show the work at readable size. Crop to the detail that matters and pair it with the full screen.
- Before and after, side by side, with the difference called out.
- Show messy artifacts (sketches, research boards) only if they explain a decision.
- Keep a consistent device frame or none at all.

## NDA and confidential work

- Ask what is allowed before publishing. Blur or replace sensitive data with realistic fakes, and say that data is illustrative.
- Describe the problem and process at a level that does not reveal confidential details; the thinking is what matters.
- Password protection is an option for sensitive pieces; still minimise what is shown.

## Presenting live

- Open with the outcome, then the story.
- One idea per slide; real screens, not bullet walls.
- Prepare for "why did you choose this over X?" and "what would you change?"
- Keep to time; leave room for questions.
